## 1. Introduction: The Voice of the North

### 1.1 The Oral Tradition and Memory

To understand Viking poetry is to understand a world where the written word was secondary to the spoken memory. The technical methods described herein were not merely aesthetic choices; they were mnemonic devices. In a culture where laws, genealogy, and mythological history were transmitted orally, the rigidity of the meter served as a preservation box for information. The intricate constraints of rhyme, alliteration, and syllable count ensured that if a single word was altered, the entire structure collapsed, thereby protecting the integrity of the text over generations of transmission.

### 1.2 Distinction Between Skaldic and Eddic Poetries

Before delving into the mechanics, one must distinguish between the two primary traditions. **Eddic poetry** is the older form, characterized by relatively simple meters—typically `Fornyrðislag` and `Ljóðaháttr`—and a direct, often narrative tone. It deals with mythological and heroic subjects, resembling the epic traditions of other Indo-European cultures.

**Skaldic poetry**, in contrast, is a highly sophisticated, technical art form that flourished in the Viking Age (c. 800–1050 AD). It is defined by extreme metrical complexity, dense use of **kennings** (metaphorical circumlocutions), and **heiti** (poetic synonyms). Skaldic poetry was the currency of the elite, composed for kings and jarls to immortalize their deeds. It is opaque, allusive, and intellectually demanding.

### 1.3 The Role of the Poet (Skald) in Society

The Skald was not a mere entertainer; he was a historian, a propagandist, and a magician of words. His social standing was precarious—he could be richly rewarded or violently punished for his verses. The technical mastery required was seen as a form of mana or power; to curse a king in verse was a physical attack, to praise him was to grant him immortality.

### 1.4 Metrical Structure as Law

In the composition of Viking verse, the meter is absolute. Unlike “free verse” or modern “slam poetry,” Viking verse is rule-governed. Every syllable has a place, every sound must resonate with its counterpart. The following sections dissect these rules with the precision of a mathematical formula.

## 2. The Sounds of Language: Phonology and Orthography

### 2.1 Old Norse Phonetics for Poets

To write authentic Viking poetry, one must engage with the phonology of Old Norse (ON). The orthography used in modern scholarship is normalized, but the poet must hear the sounds.

* **Vowels:** The vowel system is complex, featuring distinctions between short and long vowels, as well as nasalization.
  + *Short vowels:* a, e, i, o, u, ǫ (o-quality), ø, y.
  + *Long vowels:* á, é, í, ó, ú, ǫ́, ǿ, ý.
  + *Diphthongs:* au, ey, ey.
* **Consonants:** Stops (p, t, k), Fricatives (f, þ, s, h), Nasals (m, n), Liquids (l, r), and Glides (j, v).
* **Crucial Phonetic Nuances for Poetry:**
  + The distinction between voiced and unvoiced fricatives is vital for alliteration. `f` is often voiced `[v]` between vowels, but initially it is unvoiced `[f]`. `þ` (thorn) is unvoiced `[θ]`, while `ð` (eth) is voiced `[ð]`.
  + The letter `h` before `v` (as in *hvað*) is silent, allowing alliteration on `v`.

### 2.2 Alliteration: The Foundation of Verse

Alliteration (known in Old Norse as *stuðlar* or props) is the skeletal system of the poetry. In almost all meters, lines are linked by initial sounds.

* **The Rule:** Typically, the odd-numbered lines (1, 3, 5, etc.) alliterate with each other, and the even-numbered line (2, 4, 6, etc.) contains the “head-stave” (*hoðstafir*).
* **The Pattern:** Lines 1 and 3 alliterate on a specific sound. Line 2 must alliterate on that sound (the head-stave).
* **Constraint:** No line can have two heavy stresses alliterate with the same sound (e.g., you cannot have “Wolf *w*andered” if the head-stave is W). The stressed syllables must alliterate on the *first* sound of the stressed syllable.

### 2.3 Assonance and Consonance

While alliteration binds lines, internal sound patterns bind words within lines, particularly in Skaldic meters.

* **Assonance:** Repetition of vowel sounds within the line.
* **Consonance:** Repetition of consonant sounds, distinct from alliteration.

### 2.4 The Musicality of Speech

Viking poetry was sung or chanted. The rhythm is quantitative—based on the length of syllables (long vs. short)—rather than simply stress-based, though stress is the primary marker in Germanic verse. A long syllable is either long by nature (a long vowel or diphthong) or long by position (a short vowel followed by a consonant cluster or two consonants).

## 3. The Eddic Meters: The Wisdom of the Past

Eddic meters are the default for mythological wisdom (*Hávamál*) and heroic lays (*Vǫlsunga saga*). They are felt to be older and closer to the common Germanic alliterative tradition.

### 3.1 Fornyrðislag (Old Verse Meter)

This is the standard narrative meter of the Poetic Edda. It is structurally identical to the Old English *Beowulf* meter and the Old Saxon *Heliand*.

* **Structure:** Each line consists of two half-lines (or verses) separated by a caesura (pause). Each half-line has two stressed syllables and a variable number of unstressed syllables.
* **Formula:** `A | A` (Line 1), `B | B` (Line 2).
* **Alliteration:** The first stressed syllable of the second half-line (the head-stave) must alliterate with the first or second stressed syllable of the first half-line. The second stressed syllable of the second half-line must *not* alliterate.
* **Example Pattern:**
  + Half-line 1: `x / x / x` (Stress, Stress)
  + Half-line 2: `x / x / x` (Head-stave Stress, Non-alliterating Stress)
* **Constraints:**
  + There are usually 6 syllables per half-line, but this varies.
  + The first stress in the first half-line usually carries the alliteration, but the second stress can carry it if the first is a particle like “and” or “the”.

### 3.2 Málaháttr (Speech Meter)

*Málaháttr* is a variation of *Fornyrðislag*, characterized by longer lines. It is named for “speech” because it feels conversational yet elevated.

* **Structure:** Similar to *Fornyrðislag* but each half-line contains **five** stresses instead of the standard two.
* **Rhythm:** The extra syllables give it a slower, more rolling, heavy rhythm. It is often used for solemn speeches or lists of names.
* **Pattern:** `x / x / x / x / x | x / x / x / x / x`.

### 3.3 Ljóðaháttr (Song Meter)

Used primarily for poems involving magic, wisdom, and speech (like *Hávamál* and *Grímnismál*), *Ljóðaháttr* has a distinct, repetitive structure broken by a concluding “full line.”

* **Structure:**
  + Line 1: `x / x / x | x / x / x` (Two half-lines with alliteration).
  + Line 2: `x / x / x | x / x / x` (Two half-lines with alliteration).
  + Line 3: `x / x / x / x / x / x` (A single, long full line with internal rhyme or triple alliteration, often 9 syllables).
* **The “Full Line”:** The third line is the distinguishing feature. It usually has three stresses and often incorporates a cadence that emphasizes the wisdom being imparted. It stands alone, linking the previous pair to the next.

### 3.4 Galdralag (Incantation Meter)

Used specifically for spells (*galdr*), this is a variation of *Ljóðaháttr*.

* **Structure:** It follows the *Ljóðaháttr* pattern (two lines + one full line), but the “full line” is repeated.
* **Pattern:**
  + Line 1: Standard
  + Line 2: Standard
  + Line 3: Full line A
  + Line 4: Full line A (Repetition)
* **Effect:** The repetition creates a hypnotic, trance-inducing rhythm suitable for magical rites.

## 4. The Skaldic Meters: The Architecture of Complexity

Skaldic poetry represents the pinnacle of technical achievement in Old Norse verse. It is defined by intricate internal rhyme and complex syllabic rules.

### 4.1 Dróttkvætt (Court Meter)

This is the king of meters. Developed in the 9th century (often attributed to Bragi Boddason), it is the primary meter for praise poems (*drápur*).

* **Structure:**
  + Eight lines per stanza.
  + Each line has **six** syllables.
  + Three stresses per line.
* **Rhyme Scheme:**
  + Odd lines (1, 3, 5, 7) have **internal rhyme** (skothending).
  + Even lines (2, 4, 6, 8) have **full rhyme** (aðalhending).
* **Alliteration:**
  + Each pair of lines (a couplet) is linked by alliteration.
  + In the odd line (of the pair), the first stress must alliterate with the first stress of the following even line.
* **Detailed Rhyme Mechanics:**
  + *Skothending* (Odd lines): The rhyming syllables are near the end of the line. Typically, the penultimate syllable rhymes with a syllable earlier in the line. E.g., `wOrld-shAped`. (Stressed on Orld and Aped).
  + *Aðalhending* (Even lines): The last syllable of the line rhymes fully with a syllable earlier in the line. E.g., `lAnd-sAnd`.
* **Technical Constraints:**
  + Lines ending in a long syllable (masculine) vs. short syllable (feminine).
  + The rhymes must be on stressed syllables.
  + Trochaic rhythm is the norm.

### 4.2 Hrynhenda (Flowing Meter)

A variation of *Dróttkvætt*, *Hrynhenda* is often described as a “soaring” or “majestic” meter.

* **Structure:** It is *Dróttkvætt*, but with **eight** syllables per line instead of six.
* **Rhythm:** The extra syllables are usually unstressed and are typically found in the third or fourth foot. This creates a smoother, less abrupt flow than the jagged *Dróttkvætt*.
* **Rhyme:** The same rhyme rules as *Dróttkvætt* (skothending on odd, aðalhending on even) apply, but the position of the rhyming syllable shifts due to the line length.

### 4.3 Tøglag

*Tøglag* is a rare and complex meter often associated with the 10th-century poet Eyvindr Finnsson.

* **Structure:** It is characterized by lines of varying length, often alternating between 5, 6, and 7 syllables.
* **Rhyme:** It utilizes the internal rhymes of Skaldic verse but links them in a chain that runs through the stanza, creating a dense texture of sound.

### 4.4 Kviðuháttr

*Kviðuháttr* is a narrative meter used for longer poems (kviða). It is less ornate than *Dróttkvætt* but more structured than *Fornyrðislag*.

* **Structure:** Lines of seven syllables.
* **Rhyme:** It often features a simpler rhyme scheme, sometimes skipping the strict internal rhymes of *Dróttkvætt* in favor of narrative clarity.

## 5. Kenningar: The Riddle of Metaphor

The Kenning is the hallmark of Skaldic poetry. It is a circumlocution—a periphrasis—that replaces a simple noun with a poetic riddle.

### 5.1 Definition and Structure of the Kenning

A Kenning is a compound metaphor.

* **Structure:** It consists of a **Base Word** (stofn) and a **Determinant** (kenning).
* **Example:** “Sword of battle” -> The Base Word is “sword”. The Determinant is “of battle”. The meaning is “Warrior”. (Though “sword of battle” is a simple example; authentic kennings are more like “Wound-serpent” for Sword, or “Whale-road” for Sea).
* **Mechanism:** It works by substitution. A implies B, B implies C.
  + *Skull-splitter* (Axe) -> Splitter (Axe action) + Skull (Human part). Thus, the thing that splits skulls is the Axe.

### 5.2 Base Words and Determinants

* **Base Word:** This is the word that carries the grammatical gender and case of the concept being described. It is often a generic noun like “tree”, “stream”, “god”, “fire”.
* **Determinant:** This modifies the base word to restrict its meaning. It defines *which* tree, stream, god, or fire.
* **Example:***Ygg’s ash*.
  + Base: *Ash* (Tree).
  + Determinant: *Ygg’s* (Odin’s).
  + Meaning: The World Tree (Yggdrasil).

### 5.3 Types of Kennings: Simple, Extended, and Periphrastic

* **Simple Kenning:** Two words. *Wound-göt* (Wound-gosling = Arrow).
* **Extended Kenning:** Three or more words. *Wound-göt of the shield-fir* (Arrow of the warrior).
* **Periphrastic (Re-)Kenning:** A Kenning inside a Kenning.
  + *The feeder of the giant’s eagle* (The provider of the eagle = Odin).
  + *The storm of the feeder of the giant’s eagle* (The battle/chaos caused by Odin).

### 5.4 Heiti: Synonyms and Poetic Diction

Distinct from Kennings, *Heiti* are specialized, archaic, or mythological names for things.

* **Function:** They provide variety and elevate the diction.
* **Examples:**
  + **Ship:** *Elgr* (Elk – referring to the keel shape), *róðr* (Rowing-thing), *skeið* (Runner).
  + **Gold:** *Gull* (standard), *rauðr* (red – referring to fire), *Sif’s hair* (mythological kenning/heiti blur).
  + **Man:** *guma*, *karl*, *þegn*.
  + **Woman:** *konur*, *mær*, *snerp*.
* **Usage:** A poet must have a vast library of *heiti* to avoid repeating simple words like “man”, “ship”, or “sword”.

## 6. The Rules of Dróttkvætt in Detail

*Dróttkvætt* is the summit of Viking poetic technique. Its rules are so precise they act as a cryptographic key. Mastering these rules is essential for authentic composition.

### 6.1 Syllabic Structure and Line Length

A *Dróttkvætt* line is a hexameter (6 syllables) with three stresses.

* **Syllable Count:** Strictly six.
* **Stress Pattern:** Trochaic or Dactylic. `/ x / x /` (Stress, Unstress, Stress, Unstress, Stress).
* **Line End:** The line must end in a stressed syllable (masculine) or an unstressed syllable followed by a stress (feminine).
  + Masculine: `/ x / x / x` (Wait, this is 7). Let’s correct.
  + Correct Masculine: `/ x / x /` (Stress, un, Stress, un, Stress). Total 5 or 6 depending on resolution.
  + Let’s use the standard notation: `S` = Stress, `u` = Unstress.
  + Standard Dróttkvætt line: `S u S u S` (5 syllables) or `S u S u S u` (6 syllables).
  + The last syllable (if 6) is usually the “fill” or “extra”.

### 6.2 The Position of Skothending (Internal Rhyme)

In the odd lines (1, 3, 5, 7), the rhyme must occur *before* the last stress.

* **Formula:** The syllable bearing the first stress (or sometimes the second) rhymes with the syllable bearing the penultimate stress.
* **Constraint:** The rhyme is typically “vocalic skothending” (rhyming the vowel only) or “consonantal” (full rhyme).
* **Position:** If the line is `S u S u S`, the rhyme is between syllable 2 and syllable 4? No.
  + Let’s trace a standard line: \*Hǫfðu \*h\*ildr \*t\*o \*h*ildr*.
  + Stresses on *h*ildr (1), *t*o (wait, to is stress?), *h*ildr (2).
  + Rhyme is usually: The syllable after the first stress rhymes with the syllable after the second stress? No.
  + **Correct Rule:** In odd lines, the rhyming syllables are the ones *preceded* by the stresses. It is a rhyme of the coda of the first and second stressed syllables?
  + Actually, the rule is: The syllable *immediately following* the first stress rhymes with the syllable *immediately following* the second stress.
  + Let’s check: \*S \*v\* S *v* S\*.
  + Example: *Dǫlingr fann dal* (The Dane found the valley). *n* and *l* do not rhyme.
  + Authentic Example: *Grár hestur* (Grey horse). *ár* and *ur*? No.
  + Let’s look at the rhyme positions: 2 and 4. Or 1 and 3 (if including the stress).
  + **Skothending Position:** The rhyme occurs on the syllables *immediately following* the first and second stressed syllables.
  + Example: *S**v**e**n**k**j**a* … *v* and *k*? No.
  + Let’s use a real example: *Hylstr hesta* (Destroyer of horses). *yl* and *a*? No.
  + **Definitive Rule:** In odd lines, the rhyme is between the last syllable of the first foot and the last syllable of the second foot.
  + Example: *D**œ**gr**er** **y**fir **l**and*. *er* and *ir* rhyme. (Positions 3 and 6). This is Aðalhending.
  + For **Skothending** (Odd lines): The rhyme is between the last syllable of the first foot and the last syllable of the third foot? No, there are only 3 feet.
  + Let’s re-verify.
  + **Skothending:** Rhyme between the 2nd and 4th syllables (indices 1 and 3 in 0-based counting).
  + **Aðalhending:** Rhyme between the 2nd and 6th syllables (indices 1 and 5).

### 6.3 The Position of Aðalhending (End Rhyme)

In the even lines (2, 4, 6, 8), the rhyme must be perfect and include the end of the line.

* **Formula:** The syllable *immediately following* the first stress rhymes with the final syllable of the line.
* **Constraint:** This is a “full rhyme”. The vowels and following consonants must match.
* **Position:** Syllable 2 rhymes with Syllable 6.

### 6.4 Complex Alliteration Patterns

The alliteration binds the couplet.

* **Pattern:** Line 1 (Odd) has `A`. Line 2 (Even) has `A` and `B`.
  + The first stress of Line 1 (A) must alliterate with the first stress of Line 2 (A).
  + The second stress of Line 2 (B) *cannot* alliterate with A.
* **Constraint:** If the first stress of Line 1 is a particle (like *ok* ‘and’), then the second stress of Line 1 alliterates with the first stress of Line 2.

### 6.5 Vocalic and Consonantal Skothending

* **Vocalic Skothending:** The rhyming syllables share the same vowel, but the following consonants may differ. (e.g., *land* and *hand*? No, that’s consonantal. *maðr* and *faðr*? Yes, *aðr*).
* **Consonantal Skothending:** The rhyming syllables share the vowel and the following consonants. (e.g., *bekkr* and *rekkr*). This is preferred in the odd lines for a tighter sound.

## 7. Niðstænga and the Darker Arts

Viking poetry was not just for praise; it was a weapon.

### 7.1 The Mechanics of Cursing in Verse

A *níð* (insult) poem was a serious legal and magical act. To compose a *níð* was to claim the target was *argr* (cowardly, effeminate, socially repugnant) or guilty of *níð* (despicable acts).

* **The Content:** The poem must explicitly detail the shame. It often accused the target of being a passive homosexual (the ultimate taboo in Viking society), cowardice in battle, or ingratitude.
* **The Form:** It could be in *Dróttkvætt* or *Fornyrðislag*, but the meter added to the binding power of the curse.

### 7.2 Visualising the Target

The ritual often involved setting up a *níðstöng* (scorn-pole). A horse’s head (or a carved image of the target) was placed on a stake, facing the target’s home.

* **The Poem’s Role:** The verses were recited over the pole to activate the magic. The technical perfection of the verse ensured the magic “stuck”.

### 7.3 The Ritual of Níðstöng

Egill Skallagrímsson’s famous curse against King Eirik and Queen Gunnhild is the archetype.

1. **Preparation:** Carve the pole. Cut runes.
2. **Composition:** Compose the *níð* verse detailing their crimes (cowardice, being a witch/subject to sorcery).
3. **Recitation:** Speak the poem aloud, forcing the *hamingja* (luck/soul) of the targets into the pole.
4. **Erection:** Place the pole facing the land to drive their luck away.

## 8. Structural Composition: Stanzas and Fits

A single stanza (*stefja*) is the building block, but poems are structures of these blocks.

### 8.1 The Helmingr (Half-Stanza)

A *Helmingr* is a group of 4 lines (half of an 8-line stanza). Many Skaldic poems exist only as isolated *helmingar* quoted in sagas. They serve as encapsulated thoughts, maxims, or abrupt descriptive bursts.

### 8.2 The Strophe and Antistrophe

In longer, formal poems (*drápur*), the structure is influenced by the *stef* (refrain).

* **Strophe:** The initial section.
* **Stef:** A recurring stanza or half-stanza that acts as a chorus.
* **Antistrophe:** The section following the *stef*.
* **Pattern:** Strophe -> Stef -> Antistrophe -> Stef.

### 8.3 The Drápa (Encomium) vs. the Flokkr (Sequence)

* **Drápa:** A formal, highly structured praise poem with a *stef* (refrain). It is the highest form of praise. Egill’s *Höfuðlausn* (Head-Ransom) is a *drápa*.
* **Flokkr:** A sequence or series of verses without a refrain. It is looser, more casual, often for narrative rather than formal praise.

### 8.4 Narrative Progression in Non-Linear Forms

Viking poetry does not always tell a story linearly (A then B then C). Instead, it is **associative**.

* **Flashbacks and Flashforwards:** A stanza might describe a specific moment in a battle, then the next stanza the aftermath, then the cause.
* **The “Nuclear” Method:** The poem focuses on a central “nuclear” event (e.g., a specific battle) and explores it through various lenses—the weapons, the weather, the gods, the emotions—before moving to the next event.

## 9. Rhetorical Devices and Stylistic Flourishes

Beyond meter and metaphor, the Skald employed specific rhetorical tricks.

### 9.1 Ofanstepping (Climax)

*Ofanstepping* refers to ascending the scale of intensity.

* **Mechanism:** A stanza begins with a mild image or statement and builds to a powerful, often violent, climax.
* **Example:** Starting with the stillness of the sea, moving to the gathering of waves, ending in the crashing of the ship and the death of the crew.

### 9.2 Nuclear Variation (Variation on a Theme)

Also known as *kyngling*.

* **Mechanism:** The poet states the same core idea three times in three different ways within a stanza or sequence.
* **Purpose:** To reinforce the idea, explore its nuances, and display technical virtuosity.

Egill represents the peak of the classical Skaldic tradition.

### 9.3 The Stylization of Battle and Sea

Viking poetry has a “house style” for describing violence and the elements.

* **Battle (The “Old Style” – *fornyrðislag*):** Gory, visceral. References to specific body parts, the color of blood, the breaking of bone.
* **Battle (The “New Style” – *Dróttkvætt*):** Highly metaphorical. Kennings for weapons (*wound-hewn*, *blood-adder*), gods driving the battle, the personification of Death or Valkyries.
* **Sea:** Often treated as a hostile road. The ship is a horse or a dragon. The waves are *wolves* or *giants*. The act of sailing is a violent struggle against nature.

## 10. Historical Evolution of the Forms

The meters did not appear fully formed; they evolved.

### 10.1 The 9th Century: Bragi Boddason and the Origins

Bragi Boddason is the oldest named Skald. His *Ragnarsdrápa* is the oldest surviving *drápa*.

* **Innovation:** He is credited with inventing the *Dróttkvætt* meter and the extensive use of kennings. Before him, verse was likely closer to *Fornyrðislag*. Bragi complexified it to suit the intricate court culture.

### 10.2 The 10th Century: The Refinement of Egil Skallagrímsson

* **Mastery:** His *Höfuðlausn* and *Sonatorrek* demonstrate flawless command of *Dróttkvætt* and deep emotional expression within rigid technical constraints.
* **Change:** He shows the form becoming more personal and introspective, while maintaining the ornate style.

### 10.3 The Christian Era: Syncretism and Adaptation

After the conversion (c. 1000 AD), the forms persisted but the content changed.

* **Content:** Skalds began to praise Christ, Mary, and Saints using the techniques of Odin-praise. *Geisli* (Ray of Light) by Eilíf Guðrúnarson is a *drápa* to St. Olaf.
* **Form:** The meters remained, but the kennings for pagan gods were adapted or allegorized. The “All-father” became the Christian God. The “Mead of Poetry” became the Eucharist or Scriptural wisdom.

## 11. Practical Guide to Composition

To write this poetry today is to solve a logic puzzle.

### 11.1 Finding the Words: Heiti Selection

You cannot simply use “man” or “sword”. You must consult a list of *heiti*.

* **Tool:** A dictionary of Old Norse poetic synonyms (e.g., *Lexicon Poeticum*).
* **Method:** Pick a base word (e.g., *Fire*). Look for *heiti*: *Logi* (Flame), *Eldr* (Fire), *Hárr* (High).
* **Integration:** Check if the *heiti* fits the meter (syllable count and stress).

### 11.2 Fitting the Meter: The Technical Puzzle

1. **Choose the Meter:** Decide if you are writing *Fornyrðislag* or *Dróttkvætt*.
2. **Establish the Alliteration:** Pick the “head-stave” for the couplet. Let’s say it’s “K”.
   * Line 1: **K**ing of **k**nights…
   * Line 2: **K**ept the **f**ortress… (Wait, Line 2 needs K on first stress).
   * Line 2: **K**ept the **l**and safe.
3. **Add the Rhyme (Skaldic):**
   * Line 1 (Odd): Needs internal rhyme.
     + *K**in**g of **k**nights* -> *in* and *i*… doesn’t rhyme.
     + Try: *K**un**g of **b**att**le* -> *ung* and *att*. No.
     + Try: *H**el**m of **sh**ields* -> *el* and *ie*. (Vocalic rhyme?).
     + Try: *S**ei**ge of **w**ar* -> *ei* and *ar*.
   * Line 2 (Even): Needs end rhyme.
     + *K**e**pt the **l**and s**afe* -> *ept* and *afe*. (Rhymes well).
4. **Refine:**
   * Line 1: *S**ei**ge of **w**ar* -> This is 4 syllables. Need 5 or 6.
   * Line 2: *K**e**pt the **l**and s**afe* -> This is 5 syllables.
   * Adjustment:
     + Line 1: *W**ea**ry of **w**ar* -> *ea* and *ar*. (Assonance).
     + Line 2: *S**ee**k the **l**and s**afe* -> *eek* and *afe*. No.
     + Line 2: *K**ee**p the **l**and s**afe* -> *eep* and *afe*. No.
     + Line 2: *F**ee**d the **l**and s**afe* -> *eed* and *afe*.
     + Line 2: *B**ea**r the **l**and s**afe* -> *ear* and *afe*.
   * This illustrates the difficulty. One must find words that alliterate, rhyme, fit the syllable count, AND make semantic sense as a Kenning.

### 11.3 Polishing the Rhyme

Authentic Skaldic poetry favors “hard” rhymes.

* **Avoid:** Assonance (vowel-only) in *Aðalhending* (end rhymes). Use full rhymes (vowel + consonant).
* **In Skothending:** Vocalic rhymes are permitted, but consonantal ones show greater skill.
* **Syllable Weight:** Ensure the rhyming syllables are stressed. Do not rhyme unstressed syllables (e.g., *apple* and *bottle* is bad unless the stress is on those syllables, which it isn’t in English).

## 12. Glossary of Technical Terms

* **Aðalhending:** Full rhyme occurring at the end of even-numbered lines in skaldic meters.
* **Drápa:** A formal praise poem with a refrain (*stef*).
* **Dróttkvætt:** “Court meter,” the most complex skaldic meter with 6 syllable lines and internal rhymes.
* **Flokkr:** A sequence or series of verses without a refrain.
* **Fornyrðislag:** “Old story meter,” the simpler narrative meter of the Poetic Edda.
* **Galdralag:** “Incantation meter,” a variation of *Ljóðaháttr* used in spells.
* **Heiti:** A poetic synonym or name used instead of a common noun.
* **Helmingr:** A half-stanza of 4 lines.
* **Hrynhenda:** “Flowing meter,” a variation of *Dróttkvætt* with 8 syllables per line.
* **Kenning:** A periphrastic metaphor consisting of a base word and a determinant.
* **Kviðuháttr:** A narrative meter with 7-syllable lines.
* **Ljóðaháttr:** “Song meter,” used for wisdom and magic poems.
* **Málaháttr:** “Speech meter,” a variation of *Fornyrðislag* with longer lines.
* **Nið:** An insult or satire poem, often with magical implications.
* **Niðstöng:** A scorn-pole used in cursing rituals.
* **Skald:** A composer of skaldic poetry.
* **Skothending:** Internal rhyme occurring in odd-numbered lines in skaldic meters.
* **Stef:** A refrain or chorus in a *drápa*.
* **Stuðlar:** The props or alliterating staves.
* **Tøglag:** A complex, rare skaldic meter.
