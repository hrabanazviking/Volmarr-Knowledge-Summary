## *How Modern Paganism Lost the Thread of Relationship*

*(And How to Get It Back Without Becoming a Reenactment Nerd)*

---

### **A Quick Story Before We Begin**

Picture two Vikings.

**Viking A** wakes up. Pours some mead on the ground for the land-spirits. Chats with his house-carl about last night’s dreams. Goes fishing. Makes an offering to Njord because the herring are running. Shares dinner with neighbors—some of whom follow Christ, some who follow Thor, some who aren’t sure what they follow but make excellent cheese. Falls asleep listening to the wind in the fjord.

**Viking B** wakes up. Checks his ancestry DNA test to confirm he’s 23% Scandinavian. Gets angry on the internet because someone called Odin “All-Father” instead of using the “correct” Old Norse pronunciation he looked up yesterday. Yells at a stranger for combining rune magic with crystal healing. Goes to bed alone, clutching his reproduction sword, dreaming of a pure past that never existed.

One of these Vikings is practicing a living religion.

The other is building a bunker.

This article is about how we got from A to B—and how to get back.

---

## **Part I: The Web That Was**

### *(Or: Your Ancestors Were Way More Chill Than You Think)*

Let’s start with a weird claim that happens to be true:

**Historical pagans didn’t really do “religion” the way we think of it.**

They didn’t have scripture. They didn’t have Sunday school. They didn’t have doctrinal statements you had to sign to join the club. What they had was *relationships*. Lots of them. With everything.

#### **The Anthropology of “Hey, Neighbor”**

Julian Pitt-Rivers, an anthropologist who spent way too much time studying Mediterranean hospitality customs (someone had to), noticed something back in 1977. From Vedic India to Iron Age Scandinavia, Indo-European societies ran on what the Greeks called *xenia*—guest-friendship, sacred hospitality, the art of not being a jerk to strangers.

This wasn’t just being polite. It was **theological**.

The way you treated a guest was the way you treated a god. The way you treated a god was the way you treated a guest. Everything was connected through webs of obligation, gift-giving, and mutual “don’t screw this up” energy.

Mary Douglas, another anthropologist who could probably sort your entire life out just by watching how you arrange your kitchen, described small-group religions (that’s all the pre-Christian European ones) as having **weak boundaries but strong internal connections** (Douglas, 1970).

Think about that for a second.

Weak boundaries. Strong connections.

Not “who’s in and who’s out.” Not “us versus them.” Just… **connection**. Permeable. Breathing. Alive.

#### **The Land Was the Liturgy**

Richard Bradley, an archaeologist who digs things up for a living (literally), wrote a whole book called *An Archaeology of Natural Places* (2000). His big insight? Pre-Christian European religions weren’t about **believing the right things**. They were about **being in the right places**.

Sacred springs. Ancient groves. Burial mounds that whispered with ancestors. The sacred wasn’t something you carried in your head. It was something you **stood on**.

This is what philosopher Edward Casey calls “locative” spirituality (Casey, 1997). It’s the opposite of “I can worship anywhere because God is everywhere.” It’s “I worship **here** because **here** is specific, and the spirits of **here** know me.”

#### **The Norse Were Running a Relationship Startup**

Let’s look at the evidence for Norse religion specifically—because this is where modern people get really confused.

**The Landvættir: Your Local Nature Spirits, Please Don’t Scare Them**

The *Landnámabók*—basically the Icelandic settler’s handbook from way back when—has this delightful detail. When Vikings sailed to Iceland, they would **remove the dragon-prows from their ships** before landing. Why? So they wouldn’t frighten the land-spirits (Byock, 2001).

Let that sink in.

These are Vikings. We’re talking about people who raided monasteries for fun. And their first priority when reaching new land was **not freaking out the local nature spirits**.

This isn’t a religion of conquest. It’s a religion of **negotiation**. Of “hey, we’re new here, please don’t make our cows miscarry.”

**The Gods Were a Social Network**

Michael York, a historian of religion who has opinions about polytheism (and wrote a whole book called *Pagan Theology* in 2003), argues that pagan pantheons aren’t really “theologies” in the modern sense. They’re **social maps**.

The Æsir? Political gods. Chieftain energy. Leadership and law.

The Vanir? Agricultural gods. Fertility and reciprocity. “Let’s all get along and have good harvests” energy.

The Jötnar? Wild gods. Chaos and transformation. The stuff outside the boundaries that you still have to deal with.

You needed all of them. The web included everyone.

**Christianization Was a Dinner Party, Not a Crusade**

Here’s something that blows modern minds: when Scandinavia converted to Christianity, it wasn’t because missionaries won theological debates.

Anders Winroth, a historian who specializes in making Vikings more complicated than you thought, points out that Christianization happened through **political alliance and gift-exchange** (Winroth, 2014). King Óláfr Tryggvason didn’t argue people into Christianity. He **gifted** them into it. Accepted the new god as a powerful ally. Made relationships.

The idea of “faith” as believing specific propositions? That would have been **weird** to a Viking. They cared about who you sacrificed with, not what you secretly thought.

**The Blót: Religion as Potluck**

Neil Price, an archaeologist who wrote *The Viking Way* (2002) and the absolutely massive *Children of Ash and Elm* (2020), synthesized all the evidence about Norse sacrifice (*blót*). His conclusion?

It was basically a **communal barbecue with divine guests**.

You sacrificed an animal. You cooked it. You ate it together. The gods got their share. You got yours. The animal became a **mediator**—literally “making middle” between human community and divine community.

This is relationship as **shared consumption**. Not theology as **correct belief**.

See the pattern yet?

## **Part II: How the Bunker Got Built**

### *(Or: The 19th Century Ruined Everything, Part 4,729)*

So if historical paganism was all about connection, permeability, and webs of relationship… what the hell happened?

Short answer: **Romantic nationalism happened.**

Long answer: Keep reading.

#### **The Invention of “Pure” Germanicness**

In the 19th century, a bunch of German intellectuals got really into the idea that they had a **racial essence** that could be recovered from the past. This was the *Völkisch* movement—”Volk” meaning people, but in a creepy “blood and soil” way that would go on to cause **significant problems** in the 20th century.

Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke traced this whole mess in *The Occult Roots of Nazism* (1985). Mattias Gardell followed up with *Gods of the Blood* (2003). Both tell the same story: the Norse heritage got transformed from a **web of regional practices** into a **fortress of identity**.

Guido von List. The *Germanenorden*. People with impressive beards and unfortunate political ideas. They took the decentralized, localized, relational spirituality of actual Heathens and turned it into **Germanic Nationalism: The Religion**.

This was the critical turn.

From **“who are we connected to?”** (the land, the ancestors, the gods, the neighbors)

To **“what are we not?”** (not Slavic, not Jewish, not Catholic, not modern, not contaminated).

Arjun Appadurai, an anthropologist who thinks about culture professionally, calls this “culturalism” (1996)—when culture stops being the water you swim in and becomes **an object of passionate defense**. The “old ways” became a flag. A boundary marker. A **bunker**.

#### **Ásatrú: From Practice to Protest**

Fast forward to 1972. Iceland. The *Ásatrúarfélagið* gets founded. In America, the 1970s counterculture discovers Vikings. Sounds cool, right?

But here’s the thing: American Heathenry inherited that 19th-century bunker mentality.

Jennifer Snook, a sociologist who actually talked to American Heathens for her book *American Heathens* (2015), found that the movement has been **“shaped by its oppositional stance.”** Defining itself against Christianity. Against mainstream culture. Against “fluffy” Wicca. Against universalism. Against “SJWs.” Against whatever the internet is mad about this week.

This is **definitional by negation**.

A religion that knows what it **hates** but has forgotten what it **loves**.

## **Part III: The Anthropology of Why Bunkers Feel Safe**

### *(Spoiler: They’re Actually Terrible for Your Spiritual Health)*

So why do people keep building these spiritual bunkers? There’s actually anthropology for this.

#### **From Locative to Utopian to Dystopian**

Jonathan Z. Smith, a religious studies scholar who could probably deconstruct your breakfast cereal, made a famous distinction in *Map Is Not Territory* (1978). He talked about **locative** sacred spaces versus **utopian** sacred spaces.

**Locative** = centered, place-bound, specific. You are **here**. The sacred is **here**. Relationship happens **here**.

**Utopian** = unbounded, portable, anywhere. The sacred is everywhere. Or nowhere. You’re floating.

Historical paganism? Super locative. Your gods lived in **that** grove. Your ancestors were buried in **that** mound. You made offerings at **that** spring.

Modern reconstructed paganism? Increasingly utopian. Portable. Placeless. Practiced in living rooms and online forums.

But here’s the twist: when your spirituality becomes **oppositional**—when it’s about fighting enemies—then utopian becomes **dystopian**.

You carry the bunker with you.

You’re not in relationship with land, ancestors, and gods. You’re in relationship with **your enemies**.

#### **The Political Battlefield Is Anti-Land**

James C. Scott, a political theorist who studies how states ruin everything, argued in *Against the Grain* (2017) that **state formation** required destroying local, place-based relationships in favor of abstract categories.

Citizen. Subject. Class. Race. Party member.

The “political battlefield” isn’t just metaphorical. It’s **anti-local**. It’s where humans relate to each other as **abstractions** instead of as **embodied beings in specific places**.

David Abram, an environmental philosopher who actually pays attention to the non-human world, traced in *The Spell of the Sensuous* (1996) how abstract thought and literacy let humans **disengage** from the more-than-human world.

Modern Paganism’s turn toward political ideology—whether left-wing “activist witchcraft” or right-wing “folkish Heathenry”—is the same disengagement. The “land” becomes a **symbol** (of the nation, of the oppressed, of the volk) rather than a **presence** (this soil, these plants, this weather).

Tim Ingold, an anthropologist who wrote *Being Alive* (2011) and *The Perception of the Environment* (2000), developed the concept of **“dwelling”**—knowledge that comes from sustained, embodied engagement with a place.

Bunker mentality is the opposite.

Knowledge that comes from **disengagement**. From internet arguments. From portable identity that needs no specific ground.

#### **Even the Romans Weren’t This Bad**

Here’s a historical fact that ruins a lot of narratives: **pagan Rome didn’t conquer for religion.**

Ramsay MacMullen, a historian who actually looked at the evidence, documented in *Christianizing the Roman Empire* (1984) that Rome had **no concept of religious conversion as a state goal**. The *pax deorum*—peace of the gods—was maintained through **correct practice**, not **correct belief**.

Roman expansion? Driven by **plunder, tribute, and political domination**. Greed and power. Not theological imperative (Woolf, 1998; Ando, 2008).

Peter Heather, a historian who wrote *Empires and Barbarians* (2009), notes that Romanization was **cultural and economic**, not religious. When Romans encountered local gods, they didn’t destroy them. They said “oh, your god is like our god” and **added them to the pantheon**.

This is the **pagan pattern**: religious pluralism as default. Conquest for resources, not souls. Gods as **allies to negotiate with**, not **truths to enforce**.

The bunker mentality—”we must defend our faith against enemies”—is **Christian and Muslim in structure**, not pagan.

## **Part IV: The Gnostic Exception That Proves the Rule**

### *(Or: Christianity Was Cool Before It Got Popular)*

Quick detour. Remember how we said organized Abrahamic religions introduced the bunker mentality?

There’s an exception. Actually, the **original** Christianities were pretty punk rock.

#### **Outsider Religion vs. Imperial Religion**

Before Constantine, before Nicea, before Christianity became the Roman Empire’s official religion—there were just **Christianities**. Plural. Competing groups with weird ideas and small communities.

David Brakke’s *The Gnostics* (2010) and the Nag Hammadi scholars (Meyer, 2007; Pagels, 1979) have mapped this out. The Valentinians. The Sethians. The Thomas Christians. These groups were:

* **Small and voluntary** (not state churches)
* **About personal transformation** through direct experience (*gnosis*), not doctrinal assent
* **Rejection of worldly power** (the “god of this world” was often seen as a lower, messed-up power)
* **Inclusive across social boundaries** (women, slaves, foreigners as teachers and leaders)

Rodney Stark, a sociologist of religion, argued in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) that early Christianity grew precisely because it was **high-tension outsider religion**. It created new **fictive kinship bonds** across ethnic and class lines.

Then came **Theodosius I** and his edicts (381-391 CE).

Charles Freeman documented this in *The Closing of the Western Mind* (2003) and *A.D. 381* (2008). Christianity transformed from persecuted minority faith to **state-enforced orthodoxy** with the power to exclude, persecute, and define “heresy.”

This is bunker mentality in its pure form.

Defining Christianity by what it was **not**. Enforcing boundaries through state violence. The *cuius regio, eius religio* principle—”whose realm, his religion”—that dominated European religious politics until the Enlightenment.

Religion as **territory to conquer and defend**.

Not religion as **web of relationships**.

## **Part V: Symptoms of Bunker Mentality in Modern Paganism**

### *(A Field Guide to Recognizing When You’re in a Fortress Instead of a Forest)*

How do you know if you—or your community—has slipped into bunker mode?

Here are the warning signs, backed by actual research:

#### **Symptom 1: The “Lore-Only” Fortress**

*“If it’s not in the Eddas, it’s not real Heathenry.”*

Sounds authentic, right? Wrong.

Jörg Rüpke, a historian of Roman religion who wrote *Religion of the Romans* (2007) and *Pantheon* (2018), demonstrated that **no historical paganism was “lore-only.”** All living traditions evolve, borrow, adapt, and innovate.

Here’s the kicker: the “lore” we have?

* Snorri’s *Prose Edda*? Written in the **13th century** by a **Christian**.
* The *Poetic Edda*? Preserved fragments, written down by **Christians**.
* Saxo Grammaticus? Christian. Writing centuries after conversion.

These are **literary constructions**, not liturgical manuals. They preserve fragments of oral tradition filtered through medieval Christian frameworks (Lindow, 2002; Schjødt, 2020).

Treating them as “scripture” isn’t restoring Heathenry. It’s **Christianizing** it.

#### **Symptom 2: The Ancestry Trap**

*“You need Scandinavian DNA to honor Norse gods.”*

Historical evidence says: **nope.**

Leszek Gardela’s *Magic in the Viking Age* (2021) and the broader archaeological record show Norse religion was practiced by **diverse populations**—Sami, Finns, Balts, slaves of various origins who became part of Norse households.

The *Hávamál*—that famous wisdom poem—says: *“Cattle die, kinsmen die, but a good name never dies”* (Hávamál 76).

Emphasis on **name**. **Reputation**. **Relationship**.

Not blood.

The Norse concept of *frændskapr* (kinship) could be created through **oath, adoption, and service**—not merely birth (Byock, 1988).

#### **Symptom 3: The Political Weaponization**

Whether it’s:

* **Progressive Paganism**: Using witchcraft as “resistance” against “the patriarchy”
* **Traditionalist Paganism**: Using Heathenry as “resistance” against “modernity”

It’s the same pattern.

Patrick Deneen’s *Why Liberalism Failed* (2018) and Mark Lilla’s *The Shipwrecked Mind* (2016) document how political ideology has become a **substitute for religion** in modernity.

But the reverse is also true: **religion has become a form of political ideology**.

When your primary religious activity is **fighting enemies**, you’re practicing politics with spiritual aesthetics.

Not spirituality with political implications.

## **Part VI: Restoring the Web**

### *(Practical Steps for Getting Out of the Bunker)*

So how do we get back to Viking A and away from Viking B?

The **Heathen Third Path** offers a framework: **rooted but not closed, inclusive but not rootless**.

Here’s what that looks like in practice:

#### **Step 1: Get Locative or Get Lost**

Follow Bradley (2000) and Abram (1996). Restore **place-based** practice.

Learn the specific ecology of **your** place. Not “nature” in the abstract. **This** creek. **These** trees. **This** soil.

The spirits here know things. They remember things. They have **preferences**.

Ask them.

#### **Step 2: Make Friends With Non-Humans**

Donna Haraway’s *Staying with the Trouble* (2016) and Eduardo Kohn’s *How Forests Think* (2013) argue for **multi-species relationship**.

Your paganism should include:

* Animals
* Plants
* Fungi (they’re running the show anyway)
* Weather patterns
* The dead

Not just humans and gods.

Everything’s connected. Act like it.

#### **Step 3: Bring Back the Potluck**

Restore **commensality**—the shared meal—as the center of practice.

The *blót*. The *sumble*. Sacred consumption. Creating relationship through **eating together**.

Not theological debate. Not purity testing. **Bread and meat and mead and conversation.**

#### **Step 4: Practice Sacred Hospitality**

Restore *xenia*/*heitsugu* as a **primary virtue**.

The willingness to be **vulnerable to the stranger**. To create relationship across boundaries. To recognize that your enemy might become your guest might become your friend might become family.

This is the opposite of bunker mentality.

## **Part VII: The Tree and the Bunker**

### *(A Metaphor to Take With You)*

Let’s return to that image: **the tree versus the bunker**.

Historical paganisms were **arboreal**:

* Rooted in specific soil
* Branching in multiple directions
* Interconnected through mycorrhizal networks (look it up, trees are chatting underground)
* Alive, growing, dying, regenerating
* Permeable to wind and rain and light

Modern bunker Paganism is **architectural**:

* Defensive walls
* Enclosed space
* Static and rigid
* Designed for siege warfare
* Cut off from the surrounding ecosystem

The bunker serves **fear**:

* Fear of contamination
* Fear of impurity
* Fear of the Other

The tree serves **relationship**:

* The willingness to be changed by connection
* The risk of vulnerability
* The acceptance that growth requires decay

Roy Rappaport, an anthropologist who thought deeply about ritual, argued in *Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity* (1999) that religion evolved to **regulate relationship**—between humans, between humans and cosmos, between living and dead.

When religion becomes primarily about **regulating boundaries**—who’s in, who’s out, who’s pure, who’s contaminated—it has **inverted its function**.

## **The Question**

So here’s the test. The question to ask yourself, your community, your practice:

**Are you building a bunker, or are you growing a tree?**

The answer isn’t in what you believe.

It’s not in which books you’ve read.

It’s not in how historically accurate your costume is.

The answer is in **what you are connected to**.

And whether those connections are **growing**.

Or whether they have **already died**.
